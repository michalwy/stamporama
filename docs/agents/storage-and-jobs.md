# Storage, Background Work & Retention

Binary assets, in-process jobs, upload limits, and what the app is allowed to delete.

- Binary assets (photos, #112) sit behind the `src/lib/storage/` interface (filesystem + GCS, #138; ADR-0011; active write backend via `STAMPORAMA_STORAGE_BACKEND`, per-photo reads) — async/streaming, `resolveUrl` returns stream-or-redirect, write-one/read-many by per-row `storageBackend`. Bytes live under `STAMPORAMA_DATA_DIR` (the `stamporama-data` volume), never in `public/`; serve them through collection-scoped route handlers. Multipart uploads use route handlers, not server actions. `Photo` is polymorphic over **four** owners — `Item`, `Stamp`, `Offer` (#311, generated listing images, `kind = generated`; #313, images uploaded straight to an offer for a manual attachment, `kind = original`) and `ScanTile` (#566, a card scan's per-stamp crop, held before any copy exists) — with a `num_nonnulls(...) = 1` CHECK; deleting any owner must delete the bytes explicitly (the cascade only drops rows). An offer's photo plan is part rule-derived and part manual: `OfferPhotoAttachment` (#313) is the *plan entry* that carries the distinction, so a regeneration recomputes the collages and leaves the attachments alone (#315). An attachment is **N tiles in C columns** — `copy_photo` and `upload` being N = C = 1, and `manual_collage` (#331) the collector's own selection (`OfferPhotoAttachmentTile`, mixing copy scans and offer uploads) at a width they pick, rows never stored because the contents are explicit. In the plan's vocabulary a plain `collage` is always one the rules derived, hence `manual_collage` for the hand-built one. Sets that have gone — sold here, sold from under the offer, or held by an offer in active bidding — are dropped from the plan's inputs in `readInputs` (whole sets, never single copies), which marks the stored images out of date through the fingerprint alone; terminal offers are exempt. Attachments render through the same one-tile collage path as everything else — there is no unlabelled pass-through (#312). A tile caption is sized from the **label strip alone** and never from its own text (#337): sizing to the text made one image carry a full-height label and the next a visibly smaller one. Text that does not fit the tile's width is truncated with an ellipsis, and a *shorter* strip is what makes more of it fit. The strip itself is `labelPercent` of the **finished image's longest edge** — not of the stamp, which is what the gap still uses: every image of a listing is scaled to the same platform limit, so only a share of the image renders at one size across a collage, a single scan and a wide detail crop. It is therefore circular (the strip grows the canvas it is a share of) and `collage-layout.ts` solves for it, capping all the strips together at half the page so a many-row collage stays solvable. The column is a **float**: against the whole image the usable band is about one to two percent and a whole number cannot land inside it. A template’s `rows` × `columns` has always been **capacity, not a frame**, and #413 adds the second reading of it: `gridMode = auto` treats the pair as *bounds* and `resolveCollageColumns` picks each image’s width from the tiles it actually holds, scoring every allowed width by `empty cells + |columns − rows|` and breaking ties wider — two terms because empty cells alone always answers *one column* and squareness alone puts ten stamps into 3 + 3 + 3 + 1. #514 keeps both terms and moves them onto **pixels**: the width is scored on the canvas it would actually produce (`autoGridCost`), `empty cells` becoming the uncovered canvas area over the mean tile area — which now also counts the space a definitive leaves beside a souvenir sheet in its own row — and squareness becoming a term on the canvas's real aspect rather than its cell counts, so a page of wide detail crops is judged on the letterbox it makes. #526 then replaces the *target* of that term: a collage is looked at on a monitor and as a listing thumbnail, so the grid aims at a **landscape band** — `shapePenalty` is 0 anywhere between 4:3 and 16:9 and grows with the log distance to the nearest edge outside it — rather than at a square, which used to let a page of tall stamps settle into a hole-free portrait column. Portrait canvases are still reachable, just never preferred. `SHAPE_WEIGHT = 3` is unchanged, and still what sets the parity between a step off shape and one tile-sized hole. Two consequences worth knowing: a row wider than the tile count is no longer a candidate (the canvas shrinks to its contents, so those columns are free and would win every tie), and the sizes are read from the **photo rows** (`plannedTileSizes` + `trueScaledSizes`, `FULL_MAX_EDGE` clamp undone as in the renderer) rather than by decoding, so the grid is solved before a byte is read. Gap and label strip are deliberately left out of the scoring — the strip is a share of the very shape being chosen, and the gap shifts every candidate alike. The product is still the capacity, so grouping (#309) never learns of the mode, and `layOutCollage` is still handed one concrete number. It is seeded onto the offer like every other collage value, where **null means `fixed`** — hence it is not one of the columns the all-or-nothing collage group tests, and the fingerprint hashes it **only when it is `auto`**, or every plan already generated would report itself out of date over images unchanged by a pixel. The **upload order** over the whole plan (collages and attachments) is the collector's to set: `Offer.photoPlanOrder` is a `String[]` of stable per-image tokens (`c:<side>:<sortedItemIds>` / `a:<attachmentId>`, also written on each stored `OfferPhotoEntry.token`), an *override* the pure engine reconciles against the derived order at read time — so a manual order survives a composition change or regeneration. A reorder is **applied to the stored images** (their entries are renumbered; bytes untouched), so either list can be the drag surface and reordering never marks anything out of date. **Nothing is ever dropped from the plan**: an image the collector marked do-not-publish (`Offer.photoPlanUnpublished`) or one past the platform's `maxPhotos` is still rendered and shown, just kept out of the numbered upload run and the ZIP — and the limit fills from the front of the order, protecting nothing, because the order is the priority order. **Grouping reads the platform's photo limit** since #521 (`Offer.photoPreferSingles`, seeded from `Contact.photoPreferSingles`, on by default): a collage is how more stamps fit than a listing has slots for, so the single-copy pool is spent on *one image per copy* first and only the tail is chunked — the largest `k` with `k + ceil((N−k)/capacity)` inside the budget, the budget being `maxPhotos` less what the multi-copy sets' collages and the attachments already cost (front/back counted as it actually falls, do-not-publish costing nothing). No `maxPhotos` is no collage at all for singles. `k` is **never zero** while the pool has a copy: the first image is the marketplace's thumbnail and one stamp reads better there than a grid, so the slot is spent even when the budget cannot afford it and `overLimit` reports the overflow. Multi-copy sets are untouched — a set is one thing being sold. The tail's collages sit at the **end of the last run of singles**, the arithmetic being over the whole pool. The flag is hashed **only when on**, and the migration leaves every offer with rendered images off, so the upgrade declares nothing stale; off is exactly #309's original chunking, which is what the pre-#521 tests and fixtures pin.

- Background work runs **in-process**, started from `src/instrumentation.ts` `register()` — no extra compose service. Two shapes: *periodic* stateless sweeps (the photo orphan GC, #112) and *triggered* jobs queued in a **table** whose row is also the state the UI polls (offer photo generation, #311; ADR-0018). A queued job must be idempotent — `running` rows are requeued at boot — and enqueuing must be a no-op while a run is already queued or in flight. Pin worker state to `globalThis` so `next dev` HMR cannot stack a second interval.

- **`MAX_UPLOAD_BYTES` is 200 MB and is fitted to a card scan**, not to a photograph (#567). One constant in `photos/process.ts`, read by both upload routes, `scan-sheets.ts` and `photos.ts`, so nothing enforces a second cap — but the figure is **written down in prose too** (`docs/user-guide/inventory.md`, `collections.md`, `docs/architecture/overview.md` and four error messages), and docs that refuse what the app accepts are worse than no docs. At 60 MB it was a guard against a stray upload; a stockbook card at 1200 dpi passes that routinely, and the scan's bytes are *retained uncapped* because the cut is taken from the original, so refusing it refuses the ingest. Deliberately **one number for every upload** rather than a larger cap for sheets: two would need every route and message to say which one they meant, and the ordinary photo path never approaches either. It was also a memory bound, the whole file being buffered before `sharp` decoded it — and for a **sheet** that is no longer so (#590): a chunked upload assembles on disk and `prepareSheet` is handed a *path*, so a 200 MB card is never resident. It remains one for the ordinary photo path, which still buffers, and where a few megabytes make the question moot.

- **A card scan is uploaded in chunks, and that is an HTTP decision rather than a storage one** (#590). The 200 MB above was a promise no ordinary deployment could keep: Cloudflare caps a request body at 100 MB (200 on Business) and **nginx defaults `client_max_body_size` to 1 MB**, so a 1200 dpi card failed with a **413 the app never saw**. `MAX_UPLOAD_BYTES` is unchanged and stays where it is — it is the app's judgement about what a *card* may weigh, and it stops being a promise the deployment cannot keep once the bytes arrive in parts. **Direct-to-storage was the other candidate and is wrong here**: signed upload URLs are a GCS feature the filesystem backend has none of, so large scans would exist on one backend only — the exact thing `src/lib/storage/` exists to prevent, its point being that callers never learn where the bytes go. Chunking sits in the HTTP layer and is identical on both. The shape is open → send in order → finalize (`scan-uploads.ts`, `scan-sheets/uploads/…` routes), and the seam is that **finalize hands `uploadSheet` the same scan the single-request route handed it**: `prepareSheet`, the retained original, the `view`, the cut and the tiles are untouched. It hands it as a **path** rather than a buffer, and that is not a detail — the parts are joined by a stream copy into one file and `sharp` takes a path, so **nothing holds the card whole**. Reading the parts into an array and `Buffer.concat`ing them would have put ~400 MB live at once against the single-request path's ~200 MB, making chunking a memory regression wearing a fix's clothes; `PreparedSheet.openOriginal()` exists for the other half of it, opening the retained bytes as a stream at the moment `put` consumes them rather than materialising them to be passed along. Four decisions worth keeping. **A part never reaches the storage backend at all.** It is written on local disk under `STAMPORAMA_DATA_DIR/scan-uploads/<id>/`, whatever `STAMPORAMA_STORAGE_BACKEND` says, and only the *assembled sheet* is handed to the interface. A chunk is written once, read once and deleted, with an explicit lifecycle — finalize, abort, or the sweep — so putting it in a bucket would mean a 200 MB card going up as parts and coming straight back down seconds later to be assembled and then deleted: 400 MB of transfer and hundreds of operations for bytes that never needed to leave the machine. **This is not a local cache of a remote object — that is #591**, a separate mechanism with its own policy; a chunk was never remote, so there is nothing here to invalidate, evict or keep warm, and the two must not be merged by someone who sees a local copy of bytes and assumes a cache. It also settles what the interface does *not* grow: no `append` and no `compose`, since the backends' native ways of growing an object share nothing but a name. **The row (`ScanUpload`) is what makes an abandoned upload findable** — a directory says what is on disk but not whose it is, and a sweep trusting a listing would be one race away from deleting an upload mid-flight. **Chunks arrive in order**, so `receivedChunks` is both the progress and the index expected next — one number to resume against instead of a set to reconcile — and a part already held is **acknowledged rather than refused**, which is what makes a retry re-send the chunk and not the 200 MB file. **The chunk size is the operator's dial** (`STAMPORAMA_UPLOAD_CHUNK_KB`, default 512 KB, clamped rather than refused), told to the client at open time so a constant compiled into the client cannot ignore it; the default is *below* the strictest common proxy default rather than at it, because a chunk sized exactly at `client_max_body_size` leaves no room for the request line and headers a proxy may count with the body. Deliberately **only the sheet route** — a copy photo is a few megabytes and will never approach a proxy's limit, so `photos/uploads` keeps the plain single-request path, and the sheet's own single-request `POST` was **removed rather than kept as a fallback**, since it could only ever serve the cards small enough not to need it. Progress is **the count the server acknowledges**, not bytes handed to a socket, and it is emitted now because it exists only while the upload is in parts and threading it through later would cost far more. Its two phases are told apart on purpose: *uploading* is measurable and *preparing* — assembly plus a ~140 Mpx decode and the `view` — is not, and a determinate bar parked at 100% reads as a hang at precisely the moment the upload has succeeded, so the second phase says the word instead of inventing a fraction. **Abandoned uploads leave nothing behind** by three routes: finalize discards its own parts whatever the outcome, a cancelled upload aborts explicitly, and the sweep below backstops both.

- **Abandoned chunks are swept beside the abandoned photo drops, on their TTL and in their pass** (#590). A part-sent scan is staging in exactly the sense a dropped-but-unsaved photo is — the collector's data only once they commit to it — so `STAMPORAMA_PHOTO_UPLOAD_TTL_HOURS` is the answer already given to that question (#577's *the plumbing's hygiene → the operator's environment*), and a second variable saying the same thing about the same class of bytes would be one more number to keep in agreement. One change of shape it does need: age is measured from **`updatedAt`, the last accepted chunk**, and never from the open — a 200 MB card over a home connection can legitimately be in flight longer than the TTL, and sweeping an upload still making progress would break exactly the case the feature exists for.

- **A remote backend is fronted by a size-bounded local cache, and it serves *work* rather than delivery** (#591). With GCS the app fetched back bytes it had written seconds earlier: a card scan goes up and detection immediately reads it to propose the cut (200 MB each way), and an offer's collages re-read the same copy scans on every regeneration. It is a **read-through, write-through LRU with a size cap** and deliberately not a TTL: the objects are immutable under their key, so staleness — a TTL's whole job — does not exist here and there is nothing to invalidate, while what does need protecting, disk, is something a TTL cannot bound (twenty cards at 200 MB inside one hour is 4 GB whatever the TTL says) and a cap bounds by construction. It happens to keep the right things anyway, the access pattern being *written a moment ago* or *read a moment ago*. It lives **inside the storage interface** (`src/lib/storage/cache.ts`, wrapping a binding in `getStorage`/`getActiveStorage`), because ADR-0011's point is that callers never learn where the bytes are — and it is a **no-op on the filesystem backend**, where a copy would be a second one of every object on the same disk, doubling the figure the collector reads. **The rule that decides every call site is stated once**: populate on a read that feeds an operation (cutting, detecting, composing), never on one delivering bytes to a client. The photo-serving route is the case to watch — it runs once per thumbnail per list view, so populating from it would evict the handful of large objects the cache exists for — and `resolveUrl` is the same answer from the other side, the server never seeing those bytes at all. It is a **required argument** (`StorageAccess`, `"work" | "delivery"`) rather than a default or a property of the key, so a new call site has to answer it and every answer is greppable; `readFullBytes` in `offer-photo-generation.ts` is why it cannot be a property of the key, being called over the same photo to compose a collage (work) and to fill the ZIP a browser downloads (delivery). **Not the chunk staging** (#590): a part was never remote, has an explicit lifecycle and never reaches a backend, so it has nothing to invalidate, evict or keep warm — the two are said apart in both modules and in ADR-0011 §8 and §9, because someone will otherwise see two piles of local bytes and merge them.

- **The cache's cap is the operator's, its index is a table, and drift is allowed** (#591). `STAMPORAMA_STORAGE_CACHE_MAX_MB`, default **2048**, `0`/`off` disabling it — #577's rule (*the plumbing's hygiene → the operator's environment*) applied to a disk that is the operator's, and the default is fitted to the largest object the app stores rather than being round: a cap that cannot hold several 200 MB cards plus a collage run's sources would evict the objects it exists for. It is deliberately **not** `retention-ttl.ts`'s grammar — that answers *how long*, where `0` means *sweep now*, and this answers *how much*, where `0` means *hold nothing*. **One cap for the instance**, never one per collection: the resource is a single disk and a per-collection cap makes the total *N* × cap, while partitioning a fixed one reserves space for a collection nobody is touching. Several collections worked at once is the case *for* a global LRU, what matters then being the combined working set. **Clearing is per collection all the same** and needs no partitioning: keys are collection-scoped, so it is a delete by prefix, and the usage figure breaks down the same way while the cap stays whole. The **index is a table** (`storage_cache_entry`) and therefore survives a restart — anything else throws away the 200 MB the next detection immediately re-fetches — and it is affordable precisely because only work populates, so accesses are few and large; filesystem metadata is no substitute, `atime` being off on most modern mounts and `mtime` recording writing rather than use. **Row and file may drift and neither trusts the other**: a row whose file is gone is a *miss*, a file with no row is garbage the sweep collects, and no failure in the cache is ever allowed to reach a caller — a populate that fails re-fetches, a cache that cannot write returns the backend's bytes. Eviction is a **sweep to a low-water mark (80% of the cap)** rather than a trim on every write, so a run of writes pays for one pass; it is triggered by a populate that pushes past the cap rather than only hourly, because an hourly-only pass would leave disk bounded by *how much was written in an hour*, which for card scans is the entire problem. A pass is **scheduled, not merely serialized** (`cache-eviction-queue.ts`), and that distinction is the cap: a pass takes its total once at the start, so handing the running pass to a populate that arrives mid-pass answers *bring the cache back under the cap* with a pass that cannot see the object which just pushed it over — and the cache then sits over the cap until some later populate happens to arrive with nothing running. A caller finding a pass running therefore waits for it and gets a **fresh** one behind it, shared with everyone else who arrives meanwhile, so a burst still costs two passes and awaiting the call means *the cache has been measured since I called*. It is a real bound violation and not a test artefact — a self-hosted instance on a tight disk exceeded its cap the same way — and it surfaced as an intermittent integration failure holding cap + one object. The hourly pass (`sweepStorageCache`, beside the other three in `instrumentation-node.ts`) exists for the drift, not for the cap, and joins the same schedule. It is **shown in Settings on its own line, instance-wide, used against the cap, with a Clear action** — and pointedly *not* added to the collection's storage figure: one is how much of the collector's data is held and the other is reclaimable scratch, and a sum would tell an operator that deleting scans is how to recover space the cache gives back on its own. Emptying it is safe by construction, every object in it being re-fetchable, which is why the button has no confirmation.

- **Generated bytes are the only bytes the app ever deletes on a schedule — unless the collector asks otherwise** (#512, #578). The rule stands as written and #578 is the one place that can be asked: a retained card scan is a **source** (a stockbook cannot be re-scanned once it has been broken up), so its sweep **ships off**, is switched on per collection, and is the exception that has to be requested rather than a hole in the rule. Everything else swept on a timer is output (#137) or transient (#112). `purgeClosedOfferPhotos` is the second periodic sweep, beside #112's: an offer closed (`sold`/`withdrawn`) longer than the collection's closed-offer photo retention (7 days; `0` = next sweep, `off`/`never` = keep for ever) loses its `kind = 'generated'` photos and nothing else. The period is resolved **per collection** since #577 — `Collection.closedOfferPhotoTtlDays`, then `STAMPORAMA_CLOSED_OFFER_PHOTO_TTL_DAYS`, then the built-in default — so the sweep makes one pass per collection rather than one query across all of them, and the boot log can only state the *instance default*. The line it draws is #137's and holds everywhere: **output is disposable, a source is not** — a collage is rendered from the copies' scans against the offer's stored settings, so it can always be made again, while an original (a copy's scan, an image uploaded straight to the offer for an attachment, #313) is the only copy that exists. The **plan survives the purge** — photo settings, attachment rows, `photoPlanOrder`, `photoPlanUnpublished` — so Regenerate is the whole recovery, and it stays available on a closed offer even though everything else about one is frozen. Two smaller decisions carry weight: `Offer.closedAt` exists because `state` cannot say *how long* a listing has been over and `updatedAt` would be reset by a note typed on a sold offer, and the `OfferPhotoGeneration` row is moved to `purged` rather than deleted, because a card reading "never generated" would be telling the collector something untrue about images they uploaded to a marketplace.

- **A retention period is a setting; where it lives depends on what the number is *about*** (#577). The line is worth holding, because every new TTL reopens it otherwise. **The collector's own data → a collection setting**: how long a closed offer keeps its generated images (`Collection.closedOfferPhotoTtlDays`) is a question about *this* collection — a working one and an archive of something finished want different answers, and an environment variable can only give one for the whole instance. The scan-sheet retention that follows it (#578) went the same way and is where the pattern was shown to be a pattern: `Collection.scanSheetTtlDays`, `STAMPORAMA_SCAN_SHEET_TTL_DAYS`, the same three-step resolution in `scan-sheet-retention.ts`, validation at the one write site. Two things it settled for the next one. The **grammar is shared and the default is not** — `retention-ttl.ts` holds the one parser (`off`/`never`, `0`, else days) because two retention controls on one screen where `0` means opposite things would be a trap, while each setting brings its own built-in default, since what is safe to delete differs: seven days for a collage that Regenerate makes again, **keep for ever** for a card scan that nothing could. And the **columns stay separate** — a collector may keep card scans for ever while purging offer images weekly, which one column could not say. #578's own spec inherited #577's `0` inversion verbatim before it was corrected, which is worth knowing about any TTL text written between the two. **The plumbing's hygiene → the operator's environment**: `STAMPORAMA_PHOTO_UPLOAD_TTL_HOURS` is how long an abandoned in-flight upload sits in staging, and a collection has no opinion about that; `STAMPORAMA_GCS_SIGNED_URL_TTL_SECONDS` is infrastructure. Both stay. A moved TTL keeps its environment variable as the **instance default** rather than being migrated away from — an operator who configured one keeps exactly what they configured — and the column is **nullable, where null is the answer "use the instance's"** rather than a missing value; it is the first nullable setting on `Collection` for exactly that reason, since no column default can express *I have no opinion*. The column holds **the environment variable's own vocabulary**, parsed by the same function (`closedOfferPhotoTtlMs`), so there is no second grammar to drift: resolution is `collection ?? env ?? built-in default`, in `offer-photo-retention.ts`, the one module that names the variable. Read `0` and `off` carefully — they are easy to get backwards: **`0` purges at the next sweep** (the cutoff is `now`) and **`off`/`never` keeps for ever**. Validation lives at the single write site, because the read path is deliberately forgiving so a typo can never break a sweep.
